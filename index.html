The Future Is The
Biggest Multibagger

The 10-year Airtel Africa bull case — and why most investors are still not paying attention.

N
Nishu
Independent Research
May 19, 2025 12 min read

There is a moment in every great investment story where the numbers stop feeling like numbers and start feeling like destiny. Africa’s internet story is at that moment right now. Six hundred and forty-six million people are online across the continent today. By 2029, that number crosses one billion. And by 2035, the most credible models suggest 1.5 billion — making Africa not just the world’s youngest continent, but its most connected growth engine.

Most investors scan that headline and move on. They’ve heard the Africa story before. They remember the hype cycles, the infrastructure promises that didn’t materialize, the currencies that cratered. What they’re missing is that this time, the infrastructure is already there. The pipes are laid. What’s filling those pipes — mobile data — is growing at 21% per year, the fastest rate anywhere on Earth.

“The market is not pricing in what happens when a billion new consumers gain their first real internet connection — and the company collecting the bill is Airtel.”

Part I

The subscriber you haven’t counted yet

Every investor model for Airtel Africa starts with existing subscribers. That’s the wrong place to start. The right place to start is with the next subscriber — the one who doesn’t have a smartphone yet but will in the next 36 months. As internet penetration moves from 47% to 70% of a 1.4 billion-person continent, each new connection is almost automatically a new Airtel data subscriber. Airtel doesn’t need to win market share. The market is handing them customers.

What makes this particularly compelling is the behavioral shift already underway among the next generation of high-value users. University students across Africa — 34.5% of them, by current measurement — already exhibit what researchers classify as internet addiction: compulsive, daily, high-frequency usage patterns. This cohort is entering the workforce over the next five years. They will be the continent’s first true digital-native professional class, and they will pay for data the way urban millennials in India paid for Jio — without hesitation, every month, forever.

Why this matters for ARPU

ARPU — average revenue per user — is the metric that makes or breaks a telecom investment. In most mature markets, ARPU is flat or declining. In Africa, Airtel’s data ARPU is being pulled upward by two forces simultaneously: more users consuming more data each year, and a growing professional class moving into premium data tiers. This is the rarest of combinations in telecom — structural volume growth meeting pricing power.

Part II

Airtel Money — the hidden company inside the company

In 2007, Safaricom launched M-Pesa in Kenya. At the time, analysts called it a gimmick — a way for Kenyans without bank accounts to send small amounts of money by text message. Within a decade, M-Pesa was processing transactions worth more than 40% of Kenya’s GDP. It had become not just a payments platform but the country’s de facto financial operating system. Safaricom’s valuation, once a pure-play telco multiple, was quietly repriced as a fintech.

Airtel Money is that story, replicated — not in one country, but across fourteen. It is already operating across Nigeria, Kenya, Uganda, Tanzania, Rwanda, Zambia, Malawi, Madagascar, DRC, Niger, Chad, Gabon, Seychelles, and Comoros. The product is more mature than M-Pesa was at a comparable stage of continental rollout. And critically, it is being built on top of an internet user base that is growing at a rate M-Pesa never had to contend with.

“M-Pesa proved the model in one country. Airtel Money is running that playbook across fourteen — simultaneously.”

The planned Airtel Money IPO is the value unlock that most investors aren’t modeling. When Airtel separates and lists the fintech business, the market will be forced to apply a fintech multiple to a business currently buried inside a telco balance sheet. The precedent — from M-Pesa to Paytm to GoPay — is that this repricing is dramatic and swift. Airtel shareholders today are effectively holding fintech equity at telecom prices.

Part III

The math that does the work for you

The elegant thing about the Airtel Africa thesis is that it doesn’t require you to believe in management execution, or a clever acquisition, or a regulatory tailwind. It only requires you to believe in arithmetic. If Africa adds 450 million internet users by 2029, and Airtel retains its current share of those users, and mobile data consumption per user continues growing at 21% annually — the revenue curve practically draws itself. You don’t need a catalyst. The catalyst is time.

Compound that across a fintech separation, across a professional class entering peak earning years, across a continent that is urbanizing at a pace China managed in the 1990s, and you begin to understand why calling this a multibagger is not hyperbole. It is a consequence of the numbers, followed to their logical conclusion.

The bottom line
This is not a trade. It is a decade-long position.

The Airtel Africa bull case rests on three pillars that don’t require speculation: the demographic certainty of Africa’s internet expansion, the behavioral inevitability of data consumption growth, and the structural mispricing of a fintech business inside a telco wrapper. Investors who can hold for ten years are being offered something rare — a company where the future does most of the work.
